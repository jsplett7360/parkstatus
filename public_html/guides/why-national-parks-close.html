Guide · Closures

Why national parks close.

Weather, wildfire, wildlife, construction, emergencies, and shutdowns — and why most closures affect only part of a park.

Most closures aren't dramatic and rarely affect a whole park. A single trail, road, campground, or overlook shutting down is far more common than an entire site going dark. Knowing the category helps you judge whether a closure actually changes your plans.

Weather and seasonal access

High-elevation roads close for snow, sometimes for months. Desert parks restrict access during extreme heat. Flooding shuts low water crossings and canyon trails on short notice. Many of these are scheduled and predictable — a park's operating hours already account for them.

Wildfire

Active fire, smoke, and firefighting operations close areas fast and can expand day to day. Separately, parks issue fire restrictions or burn bans during dry spells — those limit campfires but usually don't close anything, which is why we treat them as a caution rather than a closure.

Wildlife and resource protection

Beaches close for shorebird nesting, trails close during bear activity or elk rut, and sensitive areas close to let habitat recover. These are protective and often seasonal.

Construction and maintenance

Road repaving, trailhead rebuilds, and facility upgrades close specific areas, sometimes for a full season. These are planned closures with defined end dates, even when the alert doesn't spell the date out.

Emergencies and safety

Rockfall, hazardous conditions, water main breaks, law-enforcement incidents, and search-and-rescue operations can close areas immediately and without notice.

Funding lapses

A federal government shutdown is its own category, and the messiest one — covered in depth in our shutdown guide.

Why most parks read "partially closed": a closed trail doesn't close the park. On our map, "partially closed" means part of a site — a road, area, or trail — is shut or under a hazard, while the rest is open. Always read the specific reason before you cancel a trip.
