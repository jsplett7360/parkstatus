Guide · Seasons

National parks open in winter.

All of them are open. Only some of them are worth the drive.

No national park shuts for the season the way a ski hill or a summer camp does. But "open" and "accessible" are different things in January. At the high-elevation parks, the roads that lead to everything you came to see are under several feet of snow from late autumn to early summer. At the desert and southern parks, winter is the best time to go — the heat is gone and so are the crowds.

Best in winter

These parks are fully open and at their most comfortable between November and March:

  • Death Valley — summer highs top 120 °F; winter is mild, clear, and the only sensible time to hike below sea level.
  • Big Bend — the Chisos Basin and the Rio Grande are pleasant in winter; this is peak season here, so reserve campsites early.
  • Everglades — the dry season (December–April) concentrates wildlife at the water holes and drops the mosquito count.
  • Joshua Tree — cool days for climbing and hiking; nights are cold.
  • Saguaro and Hot Springs — comfortable year-round, genuinely nice in winter.
  • Congaree — cooler air, fewer bugs, and the boardwalk is open.
  • Dry Tortugas and Gateway Arch — no seasonal access issues; the ferry to Dry Tortugas runs year-round, weather permitting.

Where winter closes the highlights

These parks stay "open," but the parts people travel for are gated by snow, usually November to late May or June:

  • Yellowstone — only the Gardiner–Cooke City road is plowed all winter. The rest of the loop closes to cars in early November; the interior reopens by snowcoach and snowmobile from mid-December, then to vehicles in stages through April and May.
  • Glacier — the alpine section of Going-to-the-Sun Road over Logan Pass is closed; typical full reopening is late June to early July.
  • Rocky Mountain — Trail Ridge Road closes mid-October and reopens around Memorial Day; the lower valleys stay accessible.
  • Yosemite — Yosemite Valley is open all year, but Tioga Road and Glacier Point Road close for winter.
  • Crater Lake and Lassen Volcanic — rim and through roads close; the parks pivot to snowshoeing and skiing.

Our road-status page tracks the seasonal opening and closing dates for the major park roads, with the historical dates for each.

What "open" means in winter

  • Reduced staffing. Visitor centers keep short hours or close midweek; entrance booths may be unstaffed (self-pay).
  • No services. Water is shut off at many facilities to prevent freezing; some restrooms lock; lodges and restaurants run limited schedules or close.
  • Carry chains. Several parks require tire chains to be in the vehicle from fall through spring, even for 4WD, and will turn you around without them.
  • Short days, real cold. Plan for a 5 p.m. sunset and check the forecast and the road status the morning you go.

Snow as the attraction

A few parks are winter destinations: Yellowstone by snowcoach to a frozen Old Faithful, Yosemite's Badger Pass ski area, and the groomed multi-use stretch of the Teton Park Road at Grand Teton. If snow is the point, these deliver.

FAQ

Are national park entrance fees waived in winter?

Only on fee-free days. In winter that's February 16 (Presidents Day) and, at the tail end of the year, November 11 (Veterans Day). See the 2026 fee-free days guide.

Can I camp in a national park in winter?

Yes, at parks with year-round campgrounds — Big Bend, Death Valley, Joshua Tree, Everglades and others. High-country campgrounds close with the roads.

How do I know a road is open before I drive out?

Check the park's live status and our road pages, which show the current verdict plus the typical seasonal window. Conditions can change within a day in winter.
