Guide · Closures

Are the parks open during a government shutdown?

Some close, some stay open without staff, a few run normally. Here's what a funding lapse really does — and how to check your park.

The short version: there is no single answer. During a funding lapse, some parks close, some stay open but unstaffed, and a few keep running on outside funding. The strip at the top of this page shows how many NPS sites are reporting closures right now — but a shutdown's effects change day by day, so always confirm your specific park.

What a shutdown actually does

When Congress doesn't pass funding, the Department of the Interior operates on a contingency plan and most National Park Service staff are furloughed. What that means on the ground has differed sharply from one shutdown to the next, and even from one park to the next in the same shutdown.

Three broad outcomes tend to happen at once across the system:

  1. Fully closed. Gates locked, roads barricaded, no entry. This is most common at parks with a single controlled entrance or where an unstaffed park would be unsafe or easily damaged.
  2. Open but unstaffed. Roads and trails stay physically accessible, but visitor centers are dark, restrooms and trash service stop, and there are few or no rangers. This is the trickiest category — the park looks open, but there's no one there if something goes wrong.
  3. Open and operating. A handful of parks stay fully open when a state government or a park partner steps in to pay for staffing, or where concession-run services continue.

Why "open but unstaffed" is the one to watch

An unstaffed park shifts every responsibility onto you. Expect no maintenance, no interpretive programs, and slow or absent emergency response. In past shutdowns, unstaffed parks saw overflowing trash, damaged resources, and search-and-rescue delays. If your park falls here, treat it like remote backcountry even if it normally isn't: carry out all waste, tell someone your plan, and don't count on cell coverage or a ranger.

A little history

The 2013 shutdown closed national parks across the board for its full 16 days. The 2018–2019 shutdown — the longest on record at 35 days — took the opposite approach, leaving many parks partially accessible without staff, which led to well-documented damage and pileups of trash at some of the busiest sites. The lesson from both: the policy choice can flip, so don't assume the last shutdown's rules apply to the next one.

How to check before you drive out

  • Start with the specific park. Our status map flags parks reporting closures, and each park links straight to its official NPS page.
  • Look for entrance and road status. A park can be "open" while its main road is gated — that detail lives in the park's own alerts.
  • Assume services are gone. During any lapse, plan as if restrooms, water, and visitor centers are unavailable, even at parks that stay accessible.
  • Check the date. Shutdown conditions change; a status from three days ago may not hold today.
Is there a shutdown right now? When a funding lapse is in effect, we post a site-wide banner and adjust affected parks directly. If you don't see a shutdown banner on the home page, no federal lapse is currently active as far as our sources show.
