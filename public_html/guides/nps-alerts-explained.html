Guide · Alerts

The four NPS alert types.

Danger, Closure, Caution, Information — what they signal, and why the wording matters more than the label.

The National Park Service posts alerts on park pages in four types. The type is supposed to signal severity, but in practice the wording matters more than the label — a closure sometimes gets filed under "Information." Here's what each is meant to mean, and how we read it.

Danger

An imminent or serious hazard: flash flooding, rockfall, dangerous water, an active incident. If a Danger alert covers where you're headed, take it at face value and change plans.

Closure

Something is inaccessible — a road, trail, area, campground, or occasionally the whole park. Closures are the alerts that most often change a trip, which is why our status logic weighs them most heavily.

Caution

A situation to be aware of but not an outright barrier: rough conditions, limited services, fire restrictions, wildlife activity. Worth reading, rarely a dealbreaker.

Information

General notices: events, temporary service changes, construction notes, seasonal reminders. Usually low-stakes — but because the NPS sometimes files real closures here, we read the text of every alert rather than trusting the type alone.

How this maps to our status: we scan each alert's wording, not just its type. A whole-park closure reads closed; a closed trail, road, or area — or an active hazard — reads partially closed; everything else reads open. More on that in how we check status.
